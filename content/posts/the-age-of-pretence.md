One of the strangest features of modern life is that we live in a society that celebrates authenticity while quietly rewarding performance. We are constantly encouraged to be ourselves, to speak our truth, to bring our authentic selves to work, and to embrace individuality. Yet, in practice, many of the environments we inhabit reward something very different. They reward conformity, social awareness, and the ability to read a room whilst instinctively understanding what can and cannot be said. The result is that many people learn to present versions of themselves that fit the expectations of the moment, rather than revealing who they genuinely are.

I became fascinated by this long before I had the words to describe it. Over the years, I found myself increasingly noticing a gap between what people said publicly and what they appeared to believe privately. The more I paid attention, the more I realised this gap was not confined to politics, social media, or corporate life. It seemed to exist almost everywhere. That being said, my own awareness of it began through something much simpler, in that one of the most surprising things I learned was that people do not always say what they mean.

Growing up, communication was generally straightforward, if someone liked you, you knew it, if someone disliked you, you knew that too. If you were being foolish, someone would tell you. If you had put on weight, that observation would arrive with little warning and even less sensitivity. Relationships could be warm, arguments could be heated, and criticism could be uncomfortable, but there was usually very little ambiguity. You rarely had to wonder what people really thought because they tended to tell you.

Arriving in the UK felt like stepping into a different communication system altogether. People were polite, courteous, and often genuinely pleasant, yet I quickly realised there was another layer sitting beneath the surface. Many conversations seemed to operate on two levels simultaneously: the words being spoken and the meaning hidden underneath them. It took me a while to decode it, for example, "you look well" did not always mean you looked well, "that's an interesting idea" was not always a compliment, "we should definitely do this again sometime" often had no intention attached to it whatsoever.

I found it confusing initially, then it became fascinating. Eventually, I began to wonder whether this tendency was confined to polite social interaction or whether it extended into something much deeper. The more I observed, the more I realised it was everywhere. I saw it in politics, in workplaces, in social circles, neighbourhoods, and professional networks. What I found the most intriguing was not dishonesty in the traditional sense, most of the people I encountered were not liars, they were not trying to deceive anyone. What fascinated me was something subtler, the performance.

Over time, I began noticing what I came to refer to as the social chameleons. These were people who seemed remarkably skilled at adapting themselves to whatever environment they occupied. They supported the right causes, used the right language, expressed the right views, and instinctively avoided the wrong ones. They were often intelligent, successful, well-liked, and socially aware. In many respects, they embodied exactly the kind of people modern society tends to reward.

Yet I often found myself asking the same question, how much of what I was seeing was genuine? And how much of it was survival? The question became increasingly difficult to ignore because I kept encountering situations where public behaviour and private beliefs did not seem to align. I would hear enthusiastic support for certain causes in meetings, only to hear entirely different opinions expressed in quieter settings. I would see people champion values publicly that they appeared to have little interest in practising privately. I would meet individuals who seemed passionate about inclusion, diversity, and social cohesion, yet whose actual lives remained remarkably insulated from people who were different from them.

What became clear was that many people were not necessarily expressing what they believed, they were expressing what was safe. This was particularly noticeable in the workplace. Modern organisations often encourage employees to bring their authentic selves to work. It is one of the most commonly repeated phrases in corporate culture. Yet many workplaces seem to reward the exact opposite. They reward conformity, they reward consensus and reward those who instinctively understand which opinions are safe to voice and which are best kept private.

The result is a culture where agreement is frequently mistaken for belief. People learn to support initiatives, language, and values not necessarily because they are convinced by them, but because opposing them carries social or professional risk. Over time, organisations begin confusing compliance with commitment and support becomes difficult to distinguish from performance. Most of this is not malicious, in fact, I believe much of it is entirely understandable. Humans naturally want to belong, want acceptance, opportunities, and to be liked. They want to avoid conflict and to avoid becoming the person in the room who thinks differently, because there is comfort in aligning yourself with the prevailing culture and safety in saying what is expected.

The problem arises when those incentives become more important than sincerity, because when people consistently suppress what they actually think in favour of what they think they should say, something important is lost. Honest disagreement disappears and authentic feedback becomes harder to find. Leaders start believing they have support they do not really have. Organisations convince themselves they are aligned when in reality, they are simply unchallenged. At first everything would appear healthy, because nobody is arguing and nobody is objecting, everyone would seem engaged and appear supportive, but beneath the surface, genuine trust would begin to fade.

The irony is that the cultures which appear most harmonious are sometimes the least honest. The absence of disagreement can look like unity when, in essence, it may simply be evidence that people no longer feel comfortable expressing what they actually think. This matters because from my perspective, progress depends on honesty and if people cannot challenge ideas, bad ideas survive longer than they should. If people cannot disagree openly, decision-makers would lose access to valuable information. If people become performers rather than participants, organisations would slowly drift away from reality. This is why I increasingly believe authenticity is not merely a personal virtue but a social necessity.

A society built on authentic disagreement can still move towards truth because uncomfortable conversations remain possible. A society built on polite pretence eventually loses interest in finding out what is true because doing so might disrupt the performance. The greatest danger is not that people disagree with one another, after all democracies, workplaces, communities, and relationships have always survived disagreement. The greater danger is that people stop expressing what they genuinely believe altogether.

In the end, when performance becomes more important than conviction, people stop asking what is right and start asking what is safe. Appearances gradually replace principles and consensus becomes more important than honesty. The performance continues because everyone understands the script, even if fewer and fewer people believe it. Everything would appear perfectly normal until we one day realise that nobody quite knows where anyone really stands, perhaps the strangest consequence of all. In a society that talks endlessly about authenticity, authenticity itself has become one of the rarest things to find.
